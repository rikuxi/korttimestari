How the Numbers Were Computed

←Simulator Hand rankings FI EN

Korttimestari's rankings page is built on precomputed tables containing the equity of every starting-hand class in an all-in situation against unknown opponents. The scenario is the same throughout: hero plus N−1 random hands, the full board is dealt, and the pot goes to the best hand (or is split into shares on a tie).

The exact method (heads-up)

Heads-up, equity is not an estimate but a finite sum: every possible board × every opponent hand. In Hold'em that is 2,118,760 boards × 990 opponent hands for each of the 169 classes; in Omaha 1,712,304 × 123,410 for each of the 16,432 classes. The computation is done in integers (wins and ties are counted as units), and the output files are written only if the sum invariants match to the bit — for example, the wins and ties summed over all classes must correspond exactly to the number of comparisons performed. The result is an exact fraction whose numerator and denominator are included in the data.

The hybrid method (all other tables)

Multiple opponents cannot be enumerated exhaustively — nor can Omaha5 even heads-up — but half of the problem can: all C(52,5) = 2,598,960 boards are enumerated, and randomness is used only for which cards are removed from the deck for the opponents. The sampling variance over boards therefore disappears entirely. The error bound comes from independent replicates: the same computation is run 8–16 times with separate random streams, and the spread of the replicates directly yields the standard error. The method was validated by running it heads-up and comparing against the exact table: the error distribution matched the reported standard errors (RMS z = 0.996).

Rank and its uncertainty

In the middle of a list the differences between neighbouring hands are often smaller than the standard error, so a bare rank number would be spuriously precise. That is why every row also has a rank range: a neighbour belongs to the range if the equity difference is less than two combined standard errors. Measured widths: Hold'em on average ~1 rank (effectively pinned), Omaha ~20–24 ranks, Omaha5 ~300–540 ranks. The tops and tails are pinned in every list.

Verification

  • The hand evaluator was compared against the poker-evaluator library on all C(52,5) = 2,598,960 five-card hands: 0 ordering conflicts.
  • Three mutually independent computation paths (the old Monte Carlo pipeline, exact enumeration, the hybrid) agree within error bounds.
  • Equity decreases monotonically as the player count grows for every hand class — over a million comparisons across the tables, zero violations.
  • Independent Monte Carlo spot checks (2–4 million iterations per hand) hit the tables at every tested player count.
  • The Omaha hand notation and classification was cross-checked against ProPokerTools' published list of 16,432 classes.

A sample finding: AATT versus AAKK

Heads-up, the best Omaha starting hand is not AAKK double-suited but AATT ds (71.53 % vs 70.68 %) — the tens block the opponent's straights more effectively than the kings, because a ten is part of five straights and a king of only two. The edge holds up to three players and flips from four onwards, where AAKK takes the lead. You can see this yourself on the rankings page by clicking a hand: the comparison shows its rank at every player count.

Honest limits

The equity numbers are accurate within the reported error bounds, but the fine ordering of long lists cannot be resolved by any realistic computation — in the middle of the Omaha5 list a hand's true rank can lie within a range of hundreds of ranks. The rank ranges state this directly. Strategy sites that disagree with one another (the same hand can be found online at 65.5–73 %) usually do not disclose their method; every number on this site is traceable to a method and an error bound.

Omaha Hi/Lo in the simulator

The simulator also supports four-card Omaha Hi/Lo (8-or-better). The pot is split in half: one half goes to the best high hand and the other to the best low hand. A qualifying low is five distinct ranks, all eight or lower, with the ace playing low — straights and flushes do not count against a low. Each half uses exactly two hole cards and three board cards, and they may be different cards. If nobody makes a qualifying low (the board must contain three distinct ranks of eight or lower), the high hand takes the whole pot.

In the results, equity is the player's average share of the pot, scoop means taking the whole pot alone, and part-pot is any shared portion (for example a quarter, when the low is split). The Hi/Lo row breaks equity down by half: the hi share includes the whole pot from rounds where no low was made, so hi + lo = equity. Exact enumeration works in Hi/Lo just as in the other game types when every player's cards are known. Precomputed preflop tables for Hi/Lo do not exist yet, so all-in preflop against unknown opponents is computed by simulation.
