How the Numbers Were Computed

Korttimestari's rankings page is built on precomputed tables containing the equity of every starting-hand class in an all-in situation against unknown opponents. The scenario is the same throughout: hero plus N−1 random hands, the full board is dealt, and the pot goes to the best hand (or is split into shares on a tie).

The exact method (heads-up)

Heads-up, equity is not an estimate but a finite sum: every possible board × every opponent hand. In Hold'em that is 2,118,760 boards × 990 opponent hands for each of the 169 classes; in Omaha 1,712,304 × 123,410 for each of the 16,432 classes. The computation is done in integers (wins and ties are counted as units), and the output files are written only if the sum invariants match to the bit — for example, the wins and ties summed over all classes must correspond exactly to the number of comparisons performed. The result is an exact fraction whose numerator and denominator are included in the data.

The hybrid method (all other tables)

Multiple opponents cannot be enumerated exhaustively — nor can Omaha5 even heads-up — but half of the problem can: all C(52,5) = 2,598,960 boards are enumerated, and randomness is used only for which cards are removed from the deck for the opponents. The sampling variance over boards therefore disappears entirely. The error bound comes from independent replicates: the same computation is run 8–16 times with separate random streams, and the spread of the replicates directly yields the standard error. The method was validated by running it heads-up and comparing against the exact table: the error distribution matched the reported standard errors (RMS z = 0.996).

Rank and its uncertainty

In the middle of a list the differences between neighbouring hands are often smaller than the standard error, so a bare rank number would be spuriously precise. That is why every row also has a rank range: a neighbour belongs to the range if the equity difference is less than two combined standard errors. Measured widths: Hold'em on average ~1 rank (effectively pinned), Omaha ~20–24 ranks, Omaha5 ~300–540 ranks. The tops and tails are pinned in every list.

Verification

  • The hand evaluator was compared against the poker-evaluator library on all C(52,5) = 2,598,960 five-card hands: 0 ordering conflicts.
  • Three mutually independent computation paths (the old Monte Carlo pipeline, exact enumeration, the hybrid) agree within error bounds.
  • Equity decreases monotonically as the player count grows for every hand class — over a million comparisons across the tables, zero violations.
  • Independent Monte Carlo spot checks (2–4 million iterations per hand) hit the tables at every tested player count.
  • The Omaha hand notation and classification was cross-checked against ProPokerTools' published list of 16,432 classes.

A sample finding: AATT versus AAKK

Heads-up, the best Omaha starting hand is not AAKK double-suited but AATT ds (71.53 % vs 70.68 %) — the tens block the opponent's straights more effectively than the kings, because a ten is part of five straights and a king of only two. The edge holds up to three players and flips from four onwards, where AAKK takes the lead. You can see this yourself on the rankings page by clicking a hand: the comparison shows its rank at every player count.

Honest limits

The equity numbers are accurate within the reported error bounds, but the fine ordering of long lists cannot be resolved by any realistic computation — in the middle of the Omaha5 list a hand's true rank can lie within a range of hundreds of ranks. The rank ranges state this directly. Strategy sites that disagree with one another (the same hand can be found online at 65.5–73 %) usually do not disclose their method; every number on this site is traceable to a method and an error bound.

The Omaha Hi/Lo ranking carries one further caveat that comes from the method itself. Equity is computed against a random hand, and a random hand qualifies for low fairly rarely. Hands whose value rests on the low half alone — typically a bare A2 with no high support — therefore rank higher on the list than they deserve against a realistic range of opponents: at a table where others also play A2 cards the low gets split and the share drops to a quarter. The numbers are exact for the question they answer, but that question is not the same as “how well does this hand play in a real game”.

What the numbers mean

The simulator shows the same rows for every player regardless of game type. The top two are frequencies — how often something happens — equity is an average, and the bottom two describe how precise the computation is.

RowWhat it tells you
Win / Scoop How often the player takes the whole pot alone. In Hi/Lo the row is labelled Scoop.
Tie / Part-pot How often the player gets a portion of the pot but not all of it. In Hold'em and Omaha that means a tie, and the row is labelled Tie. In Hi/Lo the pot usually splits without any tie, because one player wins the high half and another the low half — hence the label Part-pot there.
Equity The average share of the pot: the whole pot from deals the player takes alone, plus shared portions weighted by their size. This is the number that matters most.
SE The standard error of the simulation: how much the equity would move if the same run were repeated. It shrinks with the square root of the round count — four times the rounds halves the error.
Exact Equity with no estimation error. It appears alongside the simulation when every player's cards are known and the remaining boards can be enumerated in reasonable time — or when the answer is in a precomputed preflop table.

Equity is built from the top two rows by weighting. A hand that takes the whole pot in 25.49 % of deals and half the pot in 20.29 % has an equity of 1 × 25.49 % + 0.5 × 20.29 % = 35.64 %.

Equity and the betting decision

Equity should be compared against the share of the final pot you have to pay for yourself: EV = equity × final pot − your contribution. A bet is profitable when equity > your contribution / final pot.

When both players are all in for the same amount and there is no other money in the pot, your share of the final pot is exactly half — then, and only then, the break-even point is 50 %. If the pot already holds €50 of dead money and both players put in €100, the pot is €250 and the break-even drops to 40 %: 45 % equity is then clearly profitable. Rake moves the line the other way — at 5 % rake it rises to about 52.6 %, so 52 % equity is slightly losing.

These are all-in equities: they assume every card is dealt with no further betting. If play continues, equity alone does not decide it — position, playability and who has the harder decisions on later streets all matter.

The three extra Hi/Lo rows

For the halves of the pot Hi/Lo shows two different quantities, and they are easy to confuse. Share hi/lo is how much of the whole pot the player wins through each half; the hi share includes the whole pot from deals where no low was made, so hi + lo = equity. Wins hi/lo and Ties hi/lo are frequencies instead: how often the player takes a half outright or splits it. On all three rows the first number is hi and the second lo.

The two differ because winning the high half is worth different amounts on different boards: on a board with no qualifying low it takes the whole pot, on a low board only half. JQK2 against one random hand wins the high half 45.50 % of the time but collects only 35.64 % of the pot through it, because 54.01 % of boards allow a low. Other calculators usually show the frequencies; the equity (EV) is the same in both.

The row also tells you whether a hand's value leans high or low. That matters multiway: value resting on the low half is halved the moment another player makes the same low, while value resting on the high hand is untouched by it.

Omaha Hi/Lo in the simulator

The simulator also supports four-card Omaha Hi/Lo (8-or-better). The pot is split in half: one half goes to the best high hand and the other to the best low hand. A qualifying low is five distinct ranks, all eight or lower, with the ace playing low — straights and flushes do not count against a low. Each half uses exactly two hole cards and three board cards, and they may be different cards. If nobody makes a qualifying low (the board must contain three distinct ranks of eight or lower), the high hand takes the whole pot. Exact enumeration works in Hi/Lo just as in the other game types when every player's cards are known.

The Hi/Lo heads-up preflop table was computed exactly with the same method as the Omaha one: all C(52,5) = 2,598,960 boards and on each board all C(43,4) = 123,410 opponent hands, with the pot split into hi and low halves. Shares are counted in integer quarter-pot units, so the result is an exact fraction, and the table breaks out the hi and low half shares.

The tables for 3–6 players were computed with the hybrid method: all 2,598,960 boards are enumerated and only the opponents' card removal is sampled (32–128 configurations per board, 16 independent replicates for the standard error). Shares accumulate as integers with the whole pot as 5040 units — the number is divisible by every tie size and the quotient is even, so splitting into halves is exact too. The method was verified by running the same pipeline with two players and comparing it against the exact heads-up table: across all 16,432 hand classes and eleven quantities the largest difference was 0.03 percentage points. From seven players up, all-in preflop against unknown opponents is still computed by simulation.

The tables make visible that the low half gains value as the table fills: every extra player competes for the high half, fewer for the low. For the top-ranked AA32 (ds) the hi and lo shares are 50.0 and 22.6 per cent heads-up but practically equal six-handed (19.5 and 19.6). The ordering shows the same thing: 5432 (r) sits at rank 14,534 heads-up and at 2,038 six-handed.

Opponent hand ranges

In the unknown-opponents mode each opponent can be given a hand range: "top X %" means the best X per cent of hands in the preflop ranking for the table's player count, counted by combos (in Hold'em a pair is 6 combos, an offsuit hand 12). The rule is exactly the one used by the top-X % range chart on the rankings page, so that page shows which hands the range contains. Every opponent can have its own range — say UTG top 10 %, button top 40 %. 100 % is an ordinary random opponent.

The range follows the all-in equity ranking; it is not a real opening range. It knows nothing about position, bet sizes or playability, and in Omaha it weights AA hands more and rundowns less than an experienced player would. It is, however, a transparent and reproducible definition against which the hero's hand can be measured.

In the simulation each range opponent receives a random hand from its range on every round. With several range opponents the hands are drawn independently and the round is rejected if two hands share a card, so the joint distribution is exactly uniform over all admissible hand combinations — the same as "the cards were dealt and everyone happened to receive a hand in their range". Sequential drawing (the second avoiding the first's cards) would be subtly biased by blocker effects. Range hands are drawn before the board and the other random hands, so the size of a range does not depend on them. When the number of range hand combinations times the remaining boards is small enough (typically after the flop), the result is computed exactly by enumerating them all — just as with known hands. There are no precomputed preflop tables for ranges; those answer the question "vs. random hands".
